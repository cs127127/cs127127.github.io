This is a work of fiction inspired by two personal accounts. Names, places, dialogue, interior thoughts and connecting scenes have been invented or changed. The characters' interpretations are not presented as proven facts about real people.

Prologue

The Empty Chair

✦

The first thing Mara noticed was the woman’s face. Not merely because she was beautiful, though she was, in the polished, improbable way of porcelain dolls. It was because the woman’s expression changed at the sight of Mara. She had been laughing as she came through the café door; then their eyes met, and the laughter left her mouth before it left the rest of her.

The second thing Mara noticed was him.

Jonah was sitting at the table where he and Mara used to sit, his hand around a coffee cup, his shoulders squared toward the street. Mara knew its uneven leg, the pale burn mark on the timber surface, the view of the florist where they had once argued over whether yellow roses meant friendship or betrayal. She had not expected to find him there with the woman he had wanted since he was thirteen.

Mara smiled at her. A proper smile, not the tight, cutting sort people give one another when they want witnesses to believe they have behaved well. The woman had done nothing to her. They had never met. Mara said hello because hello was an ordinary word and she wanted, with a desperation that embarrassed her, to remain an ordinary woman doing ordinary errands on an ordinary Wednesday.

Jonah looked at his cup.

She ordered a long black and considered leaving. But the chemist had not yet opened, her sister was meeting her later, and she had spent too many years making herself small to accommodate other people’s discomfort. She took her coffee to the counter by the window. Behind her, the milk steamer screamed. Somewhere outside, a bus folded itself against the kerb.

Then Luke walked in, wearing sunglasses even though the sky was clouded, looking as if someone had invented him for the benefit of a camera.

“Help,” she said, scarcely moving her lips.

Luke took one look at the table behind her, removed his sunglasses, and smiled like a man stepping onto a stage he knew by heart.

“Darling,” he announced, “you have no idea how much trouble I’ve caused by being late.”

And just like that, the day became a story she would tell a hundred different ways, searching each time for the one version that would finally explain it.

Chapter One

The Woman Who Read Signs

✦

Before Jonah, Mara thought she understood the distinction between intuition and hope. Intuition was the quiet pressure in the chest when something was wrong. Hope was louder, more persuasive, and capable of disguising itself as nearly anything.

She made her living helping people tell the difference, or so she believed. She read cards in a small upstairs room above a dressmaker’s shop in Seabright, a seaside neighbourhood where everyone knew whose car was parked outside whose house. Her website described what she offered as spiritual guidance. Her accountant called it a consultancy. Her sister, Nina, said Mara spent her working days listening to other people and her evenings refusing to listen to herself.

Mara was forty-eight, divorced, and the mother of two nearly grown children. Her son sent photographs of construction sites more often than he called. Her daughter, living inland for university, had a habit of asking enormous questions as if she were asking for the time. Mara liked her independence. She had worked hard for it. She had a balcony with two pots of lavender, a kitchen full of chipped mugs, and enough money to be careful without being frightened. She had learned to sleep diagonally across her bed.

On Friday mornings she drank coffee at The Lantern, where the barista knew to add hot water without asking. Jonah first spoke to her there because she was laughing at a terrible advertisement in a property magazine. He looked over her shoulder, read the slogan — LIVE YOUR BEST LIFE IN TWENTY-SIX SQUARE METRES — and said, “That depends how badly your previous life was going.”

He was a salesman, and very good at it. At that time he sold cars, though he could have sold umbrellas in the desert by convincing his customers to prepare for the unlikely. His charm was not merely beauty or confidence, though he possessed both. He paid attention. If a waitress mentioned her son had a trial for a football team, he remembered the date. If a stranger was nervous, he made the first joke at his own expense.

Over the next month they became the sort of friends who were always surprised to find an hour had passed. He told her about growing up three streets from the beach, about his father’s impatience, about a schoolyard wall painted blue until the council gave up and painted it blue again. She told him about the years of her marriage in which she had confused the absence of arguments with peace.

There was nothing sudden about the intimacy. It came by accumulation: a second coffee, a walk past the bakery, a message at midnight about a song neither of them could remember the name of. His humour met hers as neatly as a key fits an old lock. He knew the same silly films, the same songs played too loudly in cars in the nineties. He finished her sentences, and worse, she began to finish his.

“There you go,” he said one morning, when they both reached for the sugar at once. “Same brain.”

“A worrying diagnosis,” she replied.

He laughed. She remembered feeling, with unusual force, that she had walked into a room she had visited long ago but forgotten.

He told her early that he lived with someone. Her name was Celia. They had been together for years. She was older than he was, steady, patient, and, in his account, the keeper of the calendar, the groceries, the household bills, the hundred invisible duties that let a life move forward. He spoke of her with gratitude that sometimes sounded suspiciously like obligation. She knew enough to be cautious. She also knew how easy it was to mistake caution for being in control.

When Mara told Nina about Jonah, her sister asked, “Is he available?”

“He’s a friend.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

“He lives with a woman.”

Nina made the noise she made when a jar refused to open. “Then perhaps you should keep your friendship somewhere with plenty of daylight.”

Mara called her cynical. Nina called Mara romantic. Both of them, in the ordinary way of sisters, were right.

At her workroom, clients often asked about twin flames: two souls, they said, divided from one fire and destined to find each other. Some imagined reunion, others separation, others the strange conviction that the person who hurt them most must therefore be the person who belonged to them. She never promised anyone a reunion. Yet privately, when Jonah laughed at something she had only just been thinking, she caught herself using the old language. Mirror. Recognition. Home.

The danger of a metaphor is that, repeated often enough, it can begin to behave like a law.

Chapter Two

The Boy on the Beach

✦

One afternoon in late summer, Jonah and Mara walked the long way home by the water. Children were digging holes in the sand. A gull stood on a rubbish bin like a self-appointed magistrate. Jonah was in a cheerful mood until a woman in a white linen shirt passed them on the promenade. Then he went quiet.

“Do you know her?” she asked.

“Not her. She just looks like someone.”

He took his time before telling Mara. The girl’s name was Isobel Hart. They had known each other from preschool through high school, and for most of those years he had loved her in the fierce and humiliating way children love people who barely notice them. She had been the girl everyone watched at parties, with pale skin and extraordinary dark eyes. He had been a skinny, angry boy trying to turn himself into someone nobody could overlook.

“She could make you feel about an inch tall,” he said. “And I used to go back for more.”

He told Mara stories in which Isobel had laughed at him, dismissed him, allowed other boys to use his longing as entertainment. Some of the stories were specific; others had grown vague with repetition. Years later, Mara would wonder which details belonged to the girl and which to the wound. At the time, she was struck by the fact that he spoke of her not in the past tense but as if she were standing, just out of sight, waiting to pronounce him adequate.

“Did you ever tell her?” she asked.

“Everyone knew.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

He kicked a shell into the foam. “I was thirteen. I was an idiot.”

His life had changed direction around that age. He had started using drugs too young, fallen in with people who made danger look like belonging, and lost years to a succession of bad decisions. There had been a sentence, a spell in prison, a rehabilitation programme, and the slow business of making a different life. He told her these things without asking her to admire him for surviving. Some days he was able to speak about them plainly. On others he performed a kind of swagger, as though the past were a rough district he had conquered single-handedly.

“My uncle says the hardest part isn’t stopping,” he told her. “It’s learning what to do when nobody’s looking.”

She thought of that sentence often. There are people who become fluent in public transformation and remain strangers to themselves in private.

He said he was clean. Mara had no reason to contradict him, and no right to turn the existence of his past into a permanent suspicion. What she did notice was how uncomfortable he became whenever an evening went unplanned. He knew how to sell a car, win a room, buy an excellent shirt, and tell a story that made people love him for five minutes. He seemed less sure how to endure an empty kitchen.

Celia, he said, made that easier. She had met him when he was rebuilding his life. She had believed him on days he had trouble believing himself.

“Do you love her?” she asked once, on the steps outside the café.

His face changed. “I owe her a lot.”

“That's not what I asked.”

He looked at her then with a terrible tenderness. “You sound like your sister.”

Around that time he began mentioning Isobel again. He had seen Isobel at the beach, years after school, and she had recognised him. She was married, he thought unhappily, and had a child. The old distance between them had changed. She wanted to talk. She noticed his body, the good clothes, the car. During a swim she had made some extravagant joke about having forgotten her underwear, a story he delivered with equal parts embarrassment and pleasure. He called her strange, then mentioned her twice more before sunset.

“Sounds as if she likes you now,” she said.

“Maybe she likes what she thinks I’ve become.”

She heard the pride underneath his complaint. It frightened her more than the woman herself.

That evening he showed her a photograph from school. He was standing at the edge of a group, his expression careful, almost defiant. Isobel was in the centre. Mara could not tell whether Isobel was laughing at him. She might have been laughing at anything. Photographs have this cruelty: they preserve an instant but never the explanation.

“You deserved kindness,” she told him.

He turned the phone face down. For a moment she thought he might cry. Instead he asked whether she wanted a lift home.

On the way, they stopped at a traffic light beneath the railway bridge. The red signal painted both their faces. She became aware of how close his hand was to hers on the console, and of how much she wanted him to move it another inch.

He did not. Neither did she.

Chapter Three

The Choice He Wouldn’t Make

✦

There are no grand announcements at the beginning of most mistakes. Often there is just a door held open too long, a conversation that should have ended, a touch that lasts an extra second.

Mara and Jonah’s friendship crossed its invisible line on a night of heavy rain. The power had gone out at The Lantern, and half the neighbourhood was crowded inside because the owner had found a battery lantern and a gas burner. When the storm passed, Jonah walked her home. They lingered under the awning outside her building, saying goodbye four times and not leaving.

“I feel as if I’ve known you forever,” he said.

She should have told him that forever was not a useful unit of time. Instead, she leaned into the warmth of his hand on her cheek, and he kissed her.

It was brief, and it changed everything. They looked at one another afterwards with the alarm of people who have opened a window in a room they thought had no air.

“I’m sorry,” he whispered.

“You need to talk to Celia.”

“I know.”

But knowledge is not action, and for several weeks he occupied the narrow gap between the two. They were careful in public and foolish in private. They met to discuss what ought to happen and found themselves speaking instead about what might. He said he felt more himself with Mara than with anyone. She believed him. He said he could imagine a life with her. She believed that, too. When she asked what he intended to do, the beautiful language dissolved.

Celia had built a home with Jonah. They had shared the cost of his hardest years. His uncle, who had known him through prison and rehab and every stubborn recovery after them, warned him against taking apart a stable relationship for an older woman with grown children and a business he did not understand. Mara did not know what frightened Jonah most: hurting Celia, disappointing his uncle, or discovering that love might demand more of him than longing ever had.

“You want me to tell you to come to me,” she said one night.

He stared at the floor. “Would you?”

“No. You’re the one who has a decision to make.”

He said he wanted her. He said he could not bear to lose their friendship. He said he was confused, a word that can describe honest pain but can also become a comfortable place to live. She had never wanted to audition against a woman who had stood beside him for years. It made no sense to call it destiny if the first requirement was that somebody else be quietly discarded.

The last of these conversations happened in his car, on a slip road near the motorway, after they had driven without deciding where to go. Traffic poured past like a river that had forgotten its banks. Jonah gripped the steering wheel. His voice was almost calm when he said, “I’m staying with Celia.”

There it was, an ordinary sentence with the weight of a door closing.

She looked at the illuminated dashboard. For months she had imagined that the moment he chose would provide certainty. Instead she felt two kinds of grief: for the future that would not exist, and for the good friendship that had existed before they asked it to become something else.

“I’m happy if that's where you believe you need to be,” she said.

He turned to her, incredulous. “You’re happy?”

“Not happy for myself. Happy that you’ve made a choice. But I won’t wait inside it.”

His mouth moved, then closed.

“I love you enough to leave you,” she said.

She meant the words. She also liked the way they sounded. It would take years to admit that eloquence can be another form of self-protection. If she could make leaving noble, perhaps it would hurt less.

“I don't want to lose you,” he said.

“You can't keep me in reserve.”

He drove her home by the highway. They sat outside Mara’s building for a long time, neither touching the other. Before she opened the door she added, “I shouldn’t have been an option in the first place.”

For days afterwards, she replayed his expression when she said she loved him enough to go. Had she frightened him by asking nothing? Had she wounded his pride by refusing to compete? In the language of twin flames, people spoke of the runner and the chaser, the one who fled and the one who pursued. It was tempting to fit them into those positions. But even then she understood that a story about souls could not excuse the simple fact of a man remaining in a relationship and a woman being left to interpret what that meant.

Mara put away the necklace he had once admired. She stopped taking the coastal route in the evenings. She went to work. She paid bills. She told Nina she had done the sensible thing.

“Are you all right?” Nina asked.

“No.”

“That,” Nina said, reaching for Mara’s hand, “is also sensible.”

Chapter Four

A Year of Ordinary Days

✦

To Mara’s surprise, they remained friends. Not perfectly, not without pauses, but for almost a year they managed a version of the companionship they had once possessed. They sent each other ridiculous photographs. Jonah asked how Mara’s daughter was settling into university. Mara told him when a woman at the dressmaker’s had insisted her cards must be marked because she knew the customer was about to move. Sometimes they sat with other friends at The Lantern, where a group conversation could protect them from the danger of speaking honestly.

Celia knew Mara. That was the part of the arrangement Mara least knew how to carry. Celia was gentle and striking in a quiet way, with silver appearing at her temples and the sort of laugh that made people nearby want to hear the joke. Mara never saw evidence for Jonah’s description of Celia as someone who merely needed him to need her. Celia was a person with clients at her accounting firm, a mother she visited every Sunday, and an impatient passion for botanical gardens. The longer Mara knew her, the harder it was to make her fit the role of obstacle.

Celia invited Mara to her birthday dinner. “You’re one of Jonah’s oldest friends around here now,” she said over the telephone. “And I like having people who can make him stop talking about work.”

Mara almost declined. Then she imagined Celia hearing a strange excuse, wondering what she had missed. Mara took a bottle of sparkling water and a small potted jasmine, and went.

The dinner was held on their back terrace beneath strings of golden lights. Celia wore a blue dress and kept asking whether anyone needed more food. Jonah moved through the guests with his professional ease until he came to where she stood by the herbs. Then he stopped as if he had unexpectedly found a step missing from a staircase.

“You came,” he said.

“Your partner invited me.”

“I know.”

“You look terrified.”

He smiled but did not quite meet Mara’s eyes. Behind him Celia was trying to carry four plates at once. Mara stepped past him to help her, and for the first time that evening she felt less like a secret than like a guest.

Later, when they were clearing glasses, Celia said, “He’s different around you.”

Mara kept her hands in the soapy water. “We’ve known each other a while.”

“Yes,” Celia said, without accusation. “I can see that.”

There are sentences that make a person wish for a fire alarm. Mara wanted to confess, apologise, explain that the line had been crossed and uncrossed and that she had done her best to respect the life Celia and Jonah had made. But Celia dried a glass and looked at her with an expression Mara could not read. Mara did not know what Jonah had told Celia. The truth belonged to more than one person, and she was afraid of making a declaration that would serve mostly to relieve her own guilt.

After that evening, Mara saw Jonah everywhere. Seabright was compact. Their homes were around the corner from one another, the supermarket stood between them, and the same chemist served half the neighbourhood. Sometimes he drove slowly past her building. Sometimes she glanced down from her balcony and found his car stopped near the traffic lights. Sometimes she saw him and he did not see her, or perhaps he pretended not to. Once they met outside the grocer’s and he stared at her shoes while asking whether she was well.

“Perfectly,” she said, which was a lie so grand it almost made him laugh.

When Mara was waiting to be seen, every ordinary coincidence had a spotlight on it. A red car became his red car. An engine slowing became a message. A glance avoided became proof of feeling. She began noticing her own mind keeping careful accounts: the number of encounters, the length of each silence, the way his shoulders changed when she entered a room.

She told herself she was simply observant. Then one Thursday she took a longer route to buy milk because she was afraid she might see him by the newsagent. She realised that the map of her neighbourhood had become a map of a person.

A client came to her that afternoon asking whether her former husband still loved her because he had watched three of her social media stories. She shuffled the cards and said, “There are many reasons someone might look. The only reliable way to know what he wants is to see what he chooses.”

She heard her own voice as if it came from the flat below.

At home she opened the windows and let the sea air move through the rooms. On her balcony, the lavender was flowering. A car went by slowly. She did not go to the railing to see whose it was.

Not that night.

Chapter Five

The Girl Who Grew Up

✦

The stories of Isobel returned before Isobel herself did. Jonah mentioned running into her at the beach, then receiving a message, then meeting her for a swim. Whenever Mara asked a practical question — Was Isobel still married? Did Isobel know he lived with Celia? — he answered vaguely and changed the subject to a compliment Isobel had paid him or some joke she had made about his car.

“Do you actually like her?” she asked once.

“She’s not the same person.”

“Neither are you.”

“That’s the point.”

He said it with such satisfaction that she understood something he did not. He had spent thirty years imagining a girl who could refuse him. Now he had a woman who might not. The prospect seemed to glitter in his mind like a trophy awarded to the child he had once been.

He told an ugly story about her reputation at school. Mara had heard versions of it before, always delivered second-hand by men who claimed to know what every girl had done with every boy. She disliked the casual ease with which a woman’s private life could be turned into public property. She asked whether he knew any of it himself.

“Everyone said so.”

“Everyone can be wrong.”

He shrugged, annoyed that she had interrupted the momentum of his tale. Mara wondered whether he had ever really seen Isobel outside the roles of angel and villain he had given her when they were children.

Some weeks later, at an opening for a local ceramics studio, Mara met a woman called Priya who had gone to school with them both. Priya remembered Jonah vividly, a boy who was brilliant at making teachers laugh and hopeless at keeping out of trouble. She remembered Isobel, too, as a popular girl under enormous pressure to remain popular, whose parents had separated loudly and whose friends could be merciless.

“She could be mean,” Priya said. “A lot of us could. But the gossip about her was horrible. Half of it came from boys she’d turned down.”

Mara felt an immediate, ungenerous disappointment. A part of her had wanted someone to confirm that Isobel was cruel enough to justify all Mara’s fear of her. She had been treating the past like evidence in a case she had already decided.

“Did she know Jonah liked her?” she asked.

“Everyone knew. He made sure of that.” Priya smiled sadly. “He used to bring her little things. It was intense. There was a party once when she told him to leave her alone, and he cried in front of everyone. It was awful for both of them.”

Mara carried that account home like a stone in her pocket. It did not cancel the hurt Jonah remembered. Nor did his hurt mean Isobel had owed him affection. Mara had been so moved by the rejected boy that she had almost forgotten the girl was allowed to say no.

When Mara and Jonah next had coffee, she did not repeat Priya’s story. She asked instead, “Would you want to know her now, even if she’d never gone to school with you?”

He spent a long time spooning the foam from his cappuccino.

“Why are you interrogating me?”

“I’m curious.”

“You’re jealous.”

She thought about lying. “A little,” she said. “But I'm also wondering if you want the person or the old feeling of winning.”

His eyes narrowed. “You always have to make a diagnosis.”

“I asked a question.”

He stood, put money on the table, and left before his coffee was finished.

For several days she was angry. Then she was ashamed of being angry because there was no agreement between them to break. He had chosen Celia. She had chosen not to wait. Yet she could feel an old injury opening at the thought that he might leave one woman not for her but for someone who had once refused him.

It wasn’t that Isobel was more desirable, more beautiful, or younger — though she was all those things in some lights. It was that Isobel represented a time before Jonah’s worst years, before prison, before rehab, before the life that Celia had helped him build and the strange friendship Mara had offered him. Mara had not dated anyone seriously since their kiss. She told herself she was selective, which was true; she also had to admit that some part of her was keeping an empty chair for him. If Isobel wanted him now, perhaps he could imagine that nothing had ever gone wrong.

She recognised that fantasy because she had one of her own. If Jonah someday chose her, perhaps all the months of uncertainty would be transformed into preparation, every humiliation made meaningful, every hurt revealed as part of a larger design.

A person can waste years trying to turn suffering into foreshadowing.

Chapter Six

The Arguments They Invented

✦

The quarrel came in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday. Mara was closing the shutters of her reading room when Jonah appeared at the bottom of the stairs. He looked unusually tired. There were bruised shadows beneath his eyes, and for a moment she wanted to ask whether he had been sleeping. Instead she said hello.

“Are you telling people I’m your twin flame?” he demanded.

The words landed so abruptly that she laughed once, thinking she must have misunderstood. “What?”

“Online. Those videos. You’re talking about me.”

For three years she had posted short readings about love, separation, grief, and the stories people told themselves about destiny. Some were called Twin Flame Tuesday because the phrase helped people find her work. None named him. Nothing in them was intended as a secret message, though she knew, with an uncomfortable start, that her experiences had sometimes given those readings their emotional force.

“I talk about patterns,” she said. “You don’t own the subject.”

“People know.”

“Who?”

He looked away. “It doesn't matter.”

“It matters if you’re accusing me of something.”

They stood on the stairs in the sharp smell of fresh paint from the dressmaker's shop. A customer on the pavement slowed, noticed their faces, and hurried away. Jonah was angry, that much was obvious. What he feared, she could not tell. Perhaps he felt exposed. Perhaps it was easier to blame her than to confront his choices. Perhaps some careless line of hers had sounded unmistakably personal to him. Two people can be sincere and still fail to understand what they have done to one another.

“You're always going on about signs,” he said. “Like everything has to mean something.”

His accusation hurt because it was not entirely false.

“Sometimes things do mean something,” she said. “But I have never named you. If something I've posted troubles you, show me and we can discuss it.”

He did not show her. He said she was making things difficult, accused her of appearing everywhere he went, and then walked away before she could reply.

Mara stood motionless until his footsteps faded. Only later, at home, did the fury arrive. They lived around the corner from each other. They used the same shops. They had the same friends. Was she supposed to avoid the main street to demonstrate that she wasn’t looking for him? On her phone she found several unfamiliar accounts that regularly watched her public videos. Some had blank pictures and strange combinations of letters for names. She wondered whether one belonged to him. She could not know. Still, the thought that he might be watching while accusing her of watching him felt unbearable.

She considered posting a carefully worded message about people who mistake professional content for private invitations. Then she imagined the cycle: he would see it, decide it referred to him, and a new quarrel would begin. She set the phone down.

For a week she worked badly. She mislaid her keys twice and forgot an appointment she had written in three places. During a reading, a young man asked whether the universe was sending him messages through his ex-girlfriend's song choices.

“Maybe,” she said before she could stop herself. Then she shook her head. “But even if a song reminds you of her, it doesn't tell you whether she wants to talk. Have you asked her?”

He said she had blocked him. She put the cards aside and told him that the kindest interpretation was to respect the boundary he had been given. He left disappointed but thanked her for not selling him a certainty.

That night she went through her old videos. In one, recorded after Jonah had ignored her outside the supermarket, she had said: When someone runs from a mirror, they are running from what they see in themselves. She remembered exactly whom she had been thinking of. She had not named him, but had she been using an audience to say things she could not say to his face? Her intentions had been mixed. She could admit that without accepting his accusation that her entire business was about him.

She recorded a new video and deleted it. Recorded another and deleted that, too. Finally she put the cards away and wrote a note on a yellow sheet of paper: A feeling is real. Its explanation may not be.

She pinned the paper beside her desk.

When she saw Jonah next, outside the chemist, he looked as if he wanted to cross the street. She let him decide. He stayed where he was, studying the traffic, while she walked past on her own side of the pavement.

No victory, no sign. Just pavement.

Chapter Seven

The Door of the Cage

✦

Celia was the one who told Mara they had separated. They met by accident at the Saturday flower market, both of them reaching for the same bunch of eucalyptus. Celia wore a green raincoat and no wedding ring, though Mara could not remember whether Celia had worn one before. Celia smiled when she saw Mara, but there was a tiredness around her mouth that made her seem older.

“How are you?” Mara asked.

“Oh,” Celia said. “It’s been a season.”

They walked to a bench overlooking the harbour, carrying their flowers like women leaving a small, fragrant ceremony. Celia told Mara that Jonah had moved out six weeks earlier. There had been no single explosion, she said, no clear villain, only years of living in a house where the future kept being postponed. He wanted freedom. Celia wanted to stop begging him to decide what freedom meant.

“I think I mistook being necessary for being loved,” she said, looking out at the water. “Perhaps he did too.”

Mara felt the shock of being given a sentence she had thought belonged to her alone.

“He owes you a lot,” Mara said, and immediately wished she hadn’t.

Celia gave a short laugh. “He does. And I owed him things. We weren't a charity arrangement, Mara. We had wonderful years. We also stopped making each other happy.”

“I’m sorry.”

“I’m sad,” Celia said. “But I don’t want you to be sorry on my behalf.”

A ferry passed, its windows shining like coins. Mara asked whether Celia would be all right. Celia said she had already booked a weekend in the mountains with a friend and might take a course in botanical illustration. Then, after a silence, she touched Mara’s sleeve.

“Whatever happened between the two of you, I hope it didn't cost you too much.”

Mara looked at Celia. Celia did not ask for a confession. She did not offer absolution. She seemed simply to have decided that she was done spending her life discovering things in fragments.

“We were close,” she said. “Closer than we should have been.”

“I know enough.”

“I should have told you.”

“Probably.” Celia looked down at the flowers. “He should have told me more things than I could count. I spent years waiting for honesty to arrive dressed like a big conversation. Mostly it arrives as the quiet admission that you knew something was wrong.”

They sat together until the wind turned cold. When they parted, Celia took half Mara’s eucalyptus because she’d forgotten to buy any, and Mara took home a new respect for the woman she had once imagined as the reason Jonah could not come to her.

News of his freedom travelled faster than he did. He was seen at the marina with one woman, at a music festival with another. The Shape of a Life

✦

Autumn returned to Seabright almost without warning. One day the air was full of salt and sunscreen; the next, the trees on the hill had begun dropping copper leaves across the pavement. The florist changed its window display. The Lantern hired a new barista who was bewildered by Luke's elaborate coffee orders. The pharmacy installed a brighter sign that made everyone look faintly ill beneath it.

Mara began running a monthly gathering in the upstairs room called The Art of Not Knowing. The name offended two of her old clients, who wanted their spiritual guide to know things, but it drew a surprising number of people who were tired of being promised certainty. The group talked about longing, grief, attachment, coincidence, and the stories their minds offered when the truth was unavailable. Mara brought cards, sometimes, and books of poems, and a bowl of smooth stones collected from the beach. Nobody had to believe in anything. They practised asking better questions.

At the first gathering she asked everyone to write down a question they had been carrying for too long. One woman wanted to know why her mother had loved her sister more. A man wanted to know whether the person who had left him regretted it. A student asked whether choosing the wrong career could ruin the rest of her life. She wrote her own question privately: What did Jonah feel when he saw her with Luke?

Then she asked them to turn the paper over and write a different question: What could you do if you never learned the answer?

The room became very quiet. Pens moved slowly. Someone began to cry. Someone else laughed in recognition. She wrote: Continue.

Celia came to the third gathering, to Mara’s great surprise. She stayed near the back and listened, then approached Mara afterwards carrying a sketchbook. She had taken the botanical illustration course. Her pages were filled with extraordinarily precise leaves and flowers, each delicate stem rendered with a patience Mara envied.

“I'm putting together a little exhibition,” she said. “I wondered whether the dressmaker might have space in her window.”

“I'm sure she'd love to.”

Celia looked around the room. “This suits you.”

“I think it does.”

They didn’t speak about Jonah. There would be time for that if either woman ever wished to, and perhaps neither would. Celia showed Mara a drawing of eucalyptus, the same kind they had divided at the flower market, and Mara felt the extraordinary generosity of a world in which two women could have been hurt by the same man and still be capable of seeing each other apart from him.

Luke's father fell ill that winter. For weeks Luke ceased to be the dazzling figure who could rescue a friend with a joke. He went to hospital, filled out forms, learned which vending machine sold acceptable coffee, and telephoned her from car parks when he was too tired to drive. She went over with soup one evening and found him sitting on the kitchen floor because, he said, the chairs required too much of him.

Mara sat beside Luke. They ate without conversation. Later he put his head on her shoulder and wept with the exhausted astonishment of a man who had forgotten he was allowed.

“You don't have to make me laugh,” she told him.

“Thank God,” he said. “I've got nothing.”

It occurred to her that she had once let him perform happiness for her benefit in the café. That day had been a kindness, but this was a better friendship: two people who could stand beside one another without making their connection into a spectacle for someone else to watch.

One Saturday, while Mara was walking home with bread, Jonah passed on the opposite pavement. He was alone. He lifted a hand, tentative but friendly, and she lifted hers. They kept walking. Mara did not know whether he and Isobel were together, whether he was lonely, whether his recovery was difficult, whether he regretted anything beyond what he had told her. The questions came and went like birds crossing the sky. She did not need to catch them.

She had once thought freedom would feel like a door slamming shut. It felt instead like the door staying open and her discovering she was not obliged to stand in it.

On the first warm day of spring Mara invited her children, Nina, Luke, Celia, and a few neighbours to a little supper on her balcony. Celia brought flowers, Luke brought too much bread, and Nina brought a tomato salad made from plants that had somehow survived her care. Lila argued with her brother about whether the sea was blue or merely reflecting the sky. Someone dropped a fork. Someone told a joke so terrible it circled back around to being funny. The small blue bowl from Mara’s pottery class held olives. It had turned out to be useful after all.

Near sunset, she stepped inside to fetch more glasses. From the kitchen she could hear the overlapping voices: people interrupting one another, making plans, asking after absent friends, calling her name. She stood still for a moment, touched by the sound of a life being lived without her having to persuade it to love her.

Then she went back outside.

Epilogue

What the Mirror Cannot Tell You

✦

A year after the café incident, Mara sat at the same outside table with her phone switched off and a notebook open in front of her. She had decided, for no mystical reason at all, to spend ten minutes paying attention.

At first very little happened. A delivery rider balanced a paper bag on the back of his bicycle. A woman with a pram held the door open for an elderly man. The barista knocked a spoon to the floor, picked it up, and disappeared into the kitchen. Across the road, a girl in a school uniform bent to retie her shoelace while her friend waited. A cloud passed across the sun and changed the colour of the buildings. Someone's dog barked twice and fell silent.

At the end of ten minutes, she looked down at her notebook. She had written only a few sentences. The page could not contain the smell of coffee, the salt caught in the breeze, the embarrassment of the barista, the tenderness of the girl who waited for her friend, or the thousands of private thoughts moving through all the people she had watched. It was a record, not a world.

Mara still did not know exactly how Jonah and Isobel had left the café that other Wednesday. She could not explain why ten minutes had passed without her noticing. Perhaps nothing stranger had happened than her becoming absorbed in photographs. Perhaps the shock of seeing them together had changed her sense of duration. She had never solved it, and the unsolved thing had become less frightening with time.

Sometimes she thought love was like that: real in its effects, difficult to measure, and dangerously easy to confuse with the explanations people gave it. Mara had loved Jonah. He had changed her. She did not have to deny either truth. But his presence did not mean her future was written, and his absence did not mean her story had stopped.

For years Mara had imagined a twin flame as the person who could show her her own soul, as if another human being were the only mirror in which she might become visible. She knew now that mirrors are limited things. They can give back people’s faces, the light behind them, the rooms they inhabit for a moment. They cannot tell anyone what another person intends, whether longing will become kindness, whether two people will choose each other on an ordinary Tuesday when nobody is watching. They cannot tell Mara who she will become after she walks away.

The sun came out again. Mara’s reflection appeared faintly in the café window, mixed with the moving street. She looked at it without searching for someone behind her shoulder. Then she closed the notebook, finished her coffee, and went to meet her sister.

There was no sign. There was simply the day, and it was enough.
